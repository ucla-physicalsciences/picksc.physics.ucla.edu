When intense laser light travels through a plasma, it can be deflected, reflected and absorbed by plasma waves that the light itself excites. These processes, which make up the nonlinear optics of plasmas, are central to inertial confinement fusion (ICF), where lasers must compress a pellet of deuterium and tritium to extreme pressures and densities to ignite fusion reactions. Laser–plasma instabilities such as stimulated Raman and Brillouin scattering, in which light scatters off electron plasma waves and ion-acoustic waves respectively, can scatter laser energy, create asymmetries that prevent the pellet from compressing evenly, and produce energetic electrons that preheat the fuel. We study how these instabilities grow and saturate, and how they can be controlled or mitigated, under conditions relevant to the National Ignition Facility and future inertial fusion energy. The same physics can also be put to use. Because plasmas are already ionized, they can withstand intensities far beyond the damage threshold of conventional optics, and Raman and Brillouin scattering can transfer energy from a long pump pulse to a short seed pulse, amplifying it to extreme powers and intensities. We are examining how to control Raman and Brillouin scattering for amplification using novel laser pulses with tailored spatiotemporal structure, such as pulses with a flying focus, whose peak intensity can move at an arbitrary velocity.
